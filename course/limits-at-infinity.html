    Section 1.5 Limits at Infinity

    In this section, we will explore limits at infinity, which describe the behavior of a function as the input grows without bound.
    🔗
    It may be that the limit does not exist at all! For example, if a function is defined as a ratio like the examples above, the denominator vanishes at our point of interest \(x=a,\) but unlike those examples the numerator does not vanish at \(x=a\) to provide cancellation, then we get an undefined expression, like \(1/0.\)
    🔗
    In such a case, we must examine the values of the functions closely in the neighborhood of \(x=a\text{.}\) If the values ``blow up" to infinity on both sides of \(x=a\text{,}\) we may say that the limit is infinity. Similarly, if the values converge to \(-\infty\) on both sides, then we may say that the limit is \(-\infty.\) But otherwise, all we can say is that the limit is undefined.
    🔗

    Example 56.

    The following examples demonstrate infinite limits and behavior near a vertical asymptote:
    🔗
    1. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -1} \frac{x+4}{(x+1)^2} = \infty\)
      🔗
      Since the numerator approaches \(3\) and the denominator is always positive and approaches \(0\text{,}\) the values are positive and become arbitrarily large near \(x = -1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -1} \frac{x-2}{(x+1)^2} = -\infty\)
      🔗
      Since the numerator approaches \(-3\) and the denominator is always positive and approaches \(0\text{,}\) all values are negative and large in magnitude near \(x = -1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -1} \frac{x-2}{x+1} = \text{undefined}\)
      🔗
      The values are negative and large to the right of \(x = -1\text{,}\) but positive and large on the left side. Because the one-sided limits do not match, the two-sided limit does not exist.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Limit at Infinity Rule.

    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{1}{x} = 0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 57. Limits at Infinity.

    Find the following limits:
    🔗
    1. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\lim_{x\to \infty}\left(1+\frac{1}{x}\right)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\lim_{x\to -\infty}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{x^2}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\lim_{x\to \infty}\sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\lim_{x\to \infty}x\sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Solution.
    1. As \(x \to \infty\text{,}\) the fraction \(\frac{1}{x} \to 0\text{.}\) Thus, \(1 + 0 = 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. As \(x \to -\infty\text{,}\) \(x^2 \to \infty\text{.}\) Dividing a constant by a large number gives \(0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Substituting \(\theta = \frac{1}{x}\text{,}\) as \(x \to \infty\text{,}\) \(\theta \to 0\text{.}\) Since \(\sin(0) = 0\text{,}\) the limit is \(0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Rewriting as \(\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin \theta}{\theta}\) where \(\theta = \frac{1}{x}\text{.}\) This is a fundamental limit equal to \(1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 58.

    Find \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{1}{(x-2)^2}\text{,}\) if it exists.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We analyze the behavior of the function as \(x\) approaches \(2\) from both sides:
    🔗
    • As \(x \to 2^-\text{,}\) the term \(x-2\) is negative but very small. Squaring it makes \((x-2)^2\) a very small positive number. Therefore, \(\frac{1}{(x-2)^2} \to \infty\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • As \(x \to 2^+\text{,}\) the term \(x-2\) is positive and very small. Squaring it again results in a very small positive number. Therefore, \(\frac{1}{(x-2)^2} \to \infty\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Since the function increases without bound from both sides, we write:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{1}{(x-2)^2} = \infty \end{equation*}
    The limit does not exist as a finite real number, but it is described as being infinite.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 59.

    Find \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 1^-} \frac{1}{(x-1)^3}\text{,}\) \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 1^+} \frac{1}{(x-1)^3}\text{,}\) and \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{1}{(x-1)^3}\text{,}\) if they exist.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We examine the behavior of the function \(f(x) = \frac{1}{(x-1)^3}\) near \(x = 1\text{:}\)
    🔗
    1. As \(x \to 1^-\text{,}\) \(x-1\) is a small negative number. Cubing a negative number keeps it negative, so \((x-1)^3\) is a very small negative value. Therefore:
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to 1^-} \frac{1}{(x-1)^3} = -\infty \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. As \(x \to 1^+\text{,}\) \(x-1\) is a small positive number. Cubing it results in a very small positive value. Therefore:
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to 1^+} \frac{1}{(x-1)^3} = \infty \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Because the left-hand limit (\(-\infty\)) and the right-hand limit (\(\infty\)) are not the same, the two-sided limit does not exist:
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{1}{(x-1)^3} = \text{DNE} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 60.

    If \(k\) is a positive rational number and \(c\) is any real number, then
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{c}{x^k} = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{c}{x^k} = 0 \end{equation*}
    provided that \(x^k\) is defined for all required values of \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 61.

    Let \(f(x) = \displaystyle\frac{3x^2+2x+1}{2x^2+5}\text{.}\) Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to \infty} f(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    To calculate this limit, we first find the highest power of \(x\) that appears in the function. In this case, it is \(2\text{,}\) showing up in both the numerator and denominator. Next, we divide every term by \(x\) raised to this highest power. In this case, we divide every term by \(x^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We get:
    \begin{align*} \lim_{x\to \infty} \frac{3x^2+2x+1}{2x^2+5} \amp = \lim_{x\to \infty} \frac{\frac{3x^2}{x^2} + \frac{2x}{x^2} + \frac{1}{x^2}}{\frac{2x^2}{x^2} + \frac{5}{x^2}}\\ \amp = \lim_{x\to \infty} \frac{3 + \frac{2}{x} + \frac{1}{x^2}}{2 + \frac{5}{x^2}}\\ \amp = \frac{3 + 0 + 0}{2 + 0}\\ \amp = \frac{3}{2} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 62.

    Evaluate the following limits:
    🔗
    1. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\lim_{x \rightarrow -\infty}\frac{2x^2-5}{3x^2+x+2}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\lim_{x \rightarrow -\infty}\frac{2x-5}{3x^4+x+2}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\lim_{x \rightarrow -\infty}\frac{2x^5-5}{3x^2+x+2}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Solution.
    1. Divide the numerator and denominator by \(x^2\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{2 - 5/x^2}{3 + 1/x + 2/x^2} = \frac{2-0}{3+0+0} = \frac{2}{3} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Divide by the highest power \(x^4\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{2/x^3 - 5/x^4}{3 + 1/x^3 + 2/x^4} = \frac{0-0}{3+0+0} = 0 \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Divide by \(x^2\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{2x^3 - 5/x^2}{3 + 1/x + 2/x^2} \end{equation*}
      Since the numerator behaves like \(2x^3\) and \(x \to -\infty\text{,}\) the limit is \(-\infty\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Summary of Limits of Rational Functions at Infinity.

    Let \(p(x)\) and \(q(x)\) be polynomial functions. To find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\text{,}\) compare the degree of the numerator (\(n\)) and the degree of the denominator (\(d\)):
    🔗
    • If \(n \lt d\text{:}\) The limit is always \(0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(n = d\text{:}\) The limit is the ratio of the leading coefficients.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(n \gt d\text{:}\) The limit is \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\) (determine the sign by checking the leading terms).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 63.

    Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x)\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x)\) if \(f(x) = \displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{9x^2+2}}{4x+3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    To evaluate these limits, we divide the numerator and denominator by \(|x| = \sqrt{x^2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For \(x \to \infty\text{,}\) we have \(x = \sqrt{x^2}\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\sqrt{9x^2+2}}{4x+3} \amp = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\sqrt{\frac{9x^2}{x^2} + \frac{2}{x^2}}}{\frac{4x}{x} + \frac{3}{x}}\\ \amp = \frac{\sqrt{9+0}}{4+0} = \frac{3}{4} \end{align*}
    🔗
    For \(x \to -\infty\text{,}\) we have \(x = -\sqrt{x^2}\) (or \(\sqrt{x^2} = -x\)). This introduces a negative sign when we divide the denominator by the radical:
    \begin{align*} \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{\sqrt{9x^2+2}}{4x+3} \amp = \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{\sqrt{9 + \frac{2}{x^2}}}{x\left(4 + \frac{3}{x}\right) \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2}}}\\ \amp = \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{\sqrt{9 + \frac{2}{x^2}}}{-\left(4 + \frac{3}{x}\right)}\\ \amp = \frac{3}{-4} = -\frac{3}{4} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 64.

    Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \left(\sqrt{x^2+x} - \sqrt{x^2-x}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This limit has the indeterminate form \(\infty - \infty\text{.}\) To evaluate it, we rationalize the expression by multiplying the numerator and the denominator by the conjugate:
    \begin{align*} \amp \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{(\sqrt{x^2+x} - \sqrt{x^2-x})(\sqrt{x^2+x} + \sqrt{x^2-x})}{\sqrt{x^2+x} + \sqrt{x^2-x}}\\ \amp = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{(x^2+x) - (x^2-x)}{\sqrt{x^2+x} + \sqrt{x^2-x}}\\ \amp = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{2x}{\sqrt{x^2+x} + \sqrt{x^2-x}} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Next, we divide the numerator and denominator by \(x\) (noting that \(x = \sqrt{x^2}\) for \(x \gt 0\)):
    \begin{align*} \amp = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{2}{\frac{\sqrt{x^2+x}}{\sqrt{x^2}} + \frac{\sqrt{x^2-x}}{\sqrt{x^2}}}\\ \amp = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{2}{\sqrt{1 + 1/x} + \sqrt{1 - 1/x}}\\ \amp = \frac{2}{\sqrt{1+0} + \sqrt{1-0}} = \frac{2}{2} = 1 \end{align*}
    🔗
