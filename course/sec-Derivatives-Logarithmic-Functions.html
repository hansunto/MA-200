    Section Derivatives of Logarithmic Functions

    In this section, we will find the derivatives of logarithmic functions. We will start with the natural logarithm and then extend our results to logarithms with other bases.
    🔗

    Theorem 181. Laws of Logarithm.

    Let \(a,M,\) and \(N\) be positive real numbers with \(a\not=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. (product rule) \(\log_aMN=\log_aM+\log_aN\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. (quotient rule) \(\log_a\left(\frac{M}{N}\right)=\log_aM-\log_aN\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. (power rule) \(\log_aM^p=p\log_a M\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    The change-of-base Property.

    \(\log_ax=\dfrac{\ln x}{\ln a}\quad (a>0,~a\not= 1)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The natural logarithm function, denoted as \(\ln(x)\text{,}\) is the inverse of the exponential function \(e^x\text{.}\) To find the derivative of \(\ln(x)\text{,}\) we can use implicit differentiation. Let \(y = \ln(x)\text{,}\) which implies that \(e^y = x\text{.}\) Differentiating both sides with respect to \(x\text{,}\) we get:
    🔗

    Subsection How to Derive the Derivative Formula

    Case 1: \(u = x\) (where \(x \gt 0\))
    🔗
    Let \(y = \ln x\text{.}\) We rewrite the logarithmic equation in its equivalent exponential form:
    \begin{equation*} e^y = x \end{equation*}
    Next, we differentiate both sides implicitly with respect to \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}(e^y) = \frac{d}{dx}(x) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} e^y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = 1 \end{equation*}
    Now we isolate \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{e^y} \end{equation*}
    Finally, substituting \(e^y = x\) back into the denominator yields the identity:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}(\ln x) = \frac{1}{x} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Case 2: \(y = \ln u\) (Chain Rule for \(u \gt 0\))
    🔗
    We apply the Calculus Chain Rule, which states:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx} \end{equation*}
    Substituting the known derivative from Case 1 where \(\dfrac{dy}{du} = \dfrac{1}{u}\text{,}\) we get:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}(\ln u) = \frac{1}{u} \cdot \frac{du}{dx} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Derivative of the Natural Logarithm Function.

    For a differentiable function \(u\) where \(u \gt 0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}(\ln u)=\frac{1}{u}\frac{du}{dx} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For \(x \neq 0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}(\ln |x|)=\frac{1}{x} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 182.

    Differentiate each of the following functions:
    🔗
    (a)
    \(y=\ln 2x\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. We apply the chain rule by setting the inner function \(u = 2x\text{.}\) The derivative is given by:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{2x} \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(2x) \end{equation*}
    Evaluating the inner derivative yields:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{2x} \cdot 2 = \frac{1}{x} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    2. Alternatively, applying the product rule of logarithms first gives:
    \begin{equation*} y = \ln 2 + \ln x \end{equation*}
    Since \(\ln 2\) is a constant, differentiating both sides directly gives:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = 0 + \frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{x} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    (b)
    \(y=\ln \left(\dfrac{2x+1}{3x}\right)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We use the properties of logarithms to expand the quotient before differentiating:
    \begin{equation*} y = \ln(2x+1) - \ln(3x) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} y = \ln(2x+1) - (\ln 3 + \ln x) = \ln(2x+1) - \ln 3 - \ln x \end{equation*}
    Next, we take the derivative of each individual term:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{2x+1} \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(2x+1) - 0 - \frac{1}{x} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2}{2x+1} - \frac{1}{x} \end{equation*}
    To find a single fractional expression, we find a common denominator:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2x - (2x+1)}{x(2x+1)} = \frac{-1}{x(2x+1)} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 183.

    (a)
    \(\log_2(x\sqrt{x^2+1})\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, apply the product rule and power rule of logarithms to fully expand the expression:
    \begin{equation*} \log_2(x\sqrt{x^2+1}) = \log_2 x + \log_2(x^2+1)^{1/2} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \log_2 x + \frac{1}{2}\log_2(x^2+1) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Next, use the base change formula to rewrite the expressions in terms of natural logarithms for easier differentiation:
    \begin{equation*} = \frac{\ln x}{\ln 2} + \frac{1}{2\ln 2}\ln(x^2+1) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now, find the derivative with respect to \(x\) using the chain rule on the second term:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}\left[ \frac{\ln x}{\ln 2} + \frac{\ln(x^2+1)}{2\ln 2} \right] = \frac{1}{x\ln 2} + \frac{1}{2\ln 2} \cdot \frac{1}{x^2+1} \cdot 2x \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \frac{1}{x\ln 2} + \frac{x}{(x^2+1)\ln 2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Combine the expressions over a common denominator:
    \begin{equation*} = \frac{(x^2+1) + x^2}{x(x^2+1)\ln 2} = \frac{2x^2+1}{x(x^2+1)\ln 2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    (b)
    \(\ln\left(\dfrac{\sqrt[3]{x^4+12}}{(x+16)\sqrt{x-3}} \right)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, completely expand the quotient, products, and fractional powers using log laws:
    \begin{equation*} = \ln\sqrt[3]{x^4+12} - \ln\left((x+16)\sqrt{x-3}\right) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \ln(x^4+12)^{1/3} - \left[ \ln(x+16) + \ln(x-3)^{1/2} \right] \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \frac{1}{3}\ln(x^4+12) - \ln(x+16) - \frac{1}{2}\ln(x-3) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Next, differentiate each isolated logarithmic term applying the chain rule where necessary:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}\left[ \frac{1}{3}\ln(x^4+12) - \ln(x+16) - \frac{1}{2}\ln(x-3) \right] \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{4x^3}{x^4+12} - \frac{1}{x+16} - \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{x-3} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \frac{4x^3}{3(x^4+12)} - \frac{1}{x+16} - \frac{1}{2(x-3)} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Derivatives of \(a^u\) and \(\log_au\).

    Every exponential function can be expressed as a power of the natural exponential function:
    \begin{equation*} a^x=e^{x\ln a} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For constants \(a \gt 0\) and \(a \neq 1\text{,}\) the derivatives are given by:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}(a^u)=a^u \ln a \frac{du}{dx} \qquad \frac{d}{dx}(\log_a u)=\frac{1}{u\ln a} \frac{du}{dx} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 184.

    Differentiate: \(y=2^x\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We apply the derivative rule for general exponential functions, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(a^x) = a^x \ln a\text{,}\) where the base is \(a = 2\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = 2^x \ln 2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Alternatively, we can rewrite the function using base \(e\) before differentiating:
    \begin{equation*} y = e^{x \ln 2} \end{equation*}
    Applying the chain rule yields:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = e^{x \ln 2} \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(x \ln 2) = e^{x \ln 2} \cdot \ln 2 \end{equation*}
    Substituting \(e^{x \ln 2} = 2^x\) back into the expression gives the identical result:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = 2^x \ln 2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 185.

    (a)
    \(y=\log_5 e^x\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We can simplify the expression first using the properties of logarithms. By bringing down the exponent \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} y = x \log_5 e \end{equation*}
    Since \(\log_5 e\) is a constant coefficient, we differentiate directly with respect to \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \log_5 e \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Alternatively, we can use the change-of-base formula to rewrite the expression in terms of natural logarithms:
    \begin{equation*} y = \frac{\ln(e^x)}{\ln 5} = \frac{x}{\ln 5} \end{equation*}
    Differentiating this linear expression gives:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{\ln 5} \end{equation*}
    Note that both forms are equivalent because \(\log_5 e = \frac{1}{\ln 5}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    (b)
    \(y=\dfrac{(x^2+1)(x+3)^{1/2}}{x-1}, \quad x \gt 1\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Given the complex product and quotient structure, we use logarithmic differentiation. First, take the natural logarithm of both sides:
    \begin{equation*} \ln y = \ln\left( \frac{(x^2+1)(x+3)^{1/2}}{x-1} \right) \end{equation*}
    Expand the right side completely using the product, quotient, and power laws of logarithms:
    \begin{equation*} \ln y = \ln(x^2+1) + \frac{1}{2}\ln(x+3) - \ln(x-1) \end{equation*}
    Next, differentiate both sides implicitly with respect to \(x\text{,}\) applying the chain rule:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2x}{x^2+1} + \frac{1}{2(x+3)} - \frac{1}{x-1} \end{equation*}
    Finally, isolate \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) by multiplying both sides by \(y\text{,}\) and substitute the original algebraic expression back in for \(y\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = y \left[ \frac{2x}{x^2+1} + \frac{1}{2(x+3)} - \frac{1}{x-1} \right] \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{(x^2+1)(x+3)^{1/2}}{x-1} \left[ \frac{2x}{x^2+1} + \frac{1}{2(x+3)} - \frac{1}{x-1} \right] \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises Exercises on Logarithmic Differentiation

    Differentiate each of the following functions with respect to its independent variable:
    🔗
    1.
    \(y=\ln (t^2)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, apply logarithmic properties to simplify the expression by bringing down the exponent:
    \begin{equation*} y = 2\ln t \end{equation*}
    Differentiating directly with respect to \(t\) yields:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dt} = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{t} = \frac{2}{t} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    2.
    \(y=\ln(\ln x)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We apply the chain rule, treating the outer function as \(\ln(u)\) where \(u = \ln x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{\ln x} \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(\ln x) \end{equation*}
    Evaluating the inner derivative yields:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{\ln x} \cdot \frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{x\ln x} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    3.
    \(y=\log(\sec \theta +\tan \theta)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Assuming \(\log\) denotes the common logarithm (base 10), we first apply the general logarithm derivative rule \(\dfrac{d}{d\theta}(\log_a u) = \dfrac{1}{u\ln a}\dfrac{du}{d\theta}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{d\theta} = \frac{1}{(\sec \theta + \tan \theta)\ln 10} \cdot \frac{d}{d\theta}(\sec \theta + \tan \theta) \end{equation*}
    Compute the derivatives of the trigonometric functions:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{d\theta} = \frac{\sec \theta \tan \theta + \sec^2 \theta}{(\sec \theta + \tan \theta)\ln 10} \end{equation*}
    Factor out \(\sec \theta\) in the numerator to cancel common terms:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{d\theta} = \frac{\sec \theta (\tan \theta + \sec \theta)}{(\sec \theta + \tan \theta)\ln 10} = \frac{\sec \theta}{\ln 10} \end{equation*}
    Note: If \(\log\) refers to the natural logarithm in your context, the \(\ln 10\) factor is omitted, yielding \(\sec \theta\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    4.
    \(y= \dfrac{1+\ln t}{1-\ln t}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We apply the quotient rule \(\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)' = \dfrac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}\) with respect to \(t\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{\dfrac{1}{t}(1-\ln t) - (1+\ln t)\left(-\dfrac{1}{t}\right)}{(1-\ln t)^2} \end{equation*}
    Factor out \(\dfrac{1}{t}\) in the numerator to simplify the terms:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{dt} = \dfrac{\dfrac{1}{t}\left[(1-\ln t) + (1+\ln t)\right]}{(1-\ln t)^2} \end{equation*}
    Combine terms to get the final simplified expression:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{dt} = \dfrac{2}{t(1-\ln t)^2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    5.
    \(y=e^{(\cos t+\ln t)}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, use exponent rules to simplify the function before differentiating:
    \begin{equation*} y = e^{\cos t} \cdot e^{\ln t} = t e^{\cos t} \end{equation*}
    Now, apply the product rule to evaluate the derivative:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dt} = 1 \cdot e^{\cos t} + t \cdot \left(e^{\cos t} \cdot (-\sin t)\right) \end{equation*}
    Factor out \(e^{\cos t}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dt} = e^{\cos t}(1 - t\sin t) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    6.
    \(\ln y=e^y \sin x\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since the equation defines \(y\) implicitly, we use implicit differentiation with respect to \(x\text{.}\) Differentiate both sides:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx} = \left(e^y \cdot \dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)\sin x + e^y(\cos x) \end{equation*}
    Gather all terms involving \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) onto the left side of the equation:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dx} - e^y \sin x \dfrac{dy}{dx} = e^y \cos x \end{equation*}
    Factor out \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{dx}\left(\dfrac{1}{y} - e^y \sin x\right) = e^y \cos x \end{equation*}
    Isolate \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{e^y \cos x}{\dfrac{1}{y} - e^y \sin x} \end{equation*}
    Multiply the numerator and denominator by \(y\) to clear the complex fraction:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{y e^y \cos x}{1 - y e^y \sin x} \end{equation*}
    🔗
