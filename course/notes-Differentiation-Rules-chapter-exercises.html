    Section 2. Differentiation Rules- Chapter Exercises

    This section contains exercises covering the foundational differentiation techniques —including the Product Rule, Quotient Rule, Chain Rule, implicit differentiation, logarithmic differentiation —and their geometric applications such as related rates problems.
    🔗

    Exercises Exercises

    1.

    Differentiate the following functions:
    1. \(\displaystyle y = x^7 + \sqrt{7}x - \frac{1}{\pi+1}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle p = 2\theta\cos\theta\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle r = \frac{1+\cos\theta}{1-\cos\theta}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\displaystyle y = \sqrt{1-u^2+\tan u}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(\displaystyle y = (8-x+3x^2)(2-9x)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    6. \(\displaystyle y = e^{\sin(x^2)}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    7. \(\displaystyle y = \ln(1-3x)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    • For (a), remember that any term without an explicit variable like \(x\) is a constant; its derivative is zero.
      🔗
      🔗
    • For (b) and (e), apply the Product Rule: \((uv)' = u'v + uv'\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • For (c), apply the Quotient Rule and simplify the numerator using trig identities.
      🔗
      🔗
    • For (d), (f), (g), and (h), apply the Chain Rule: \(\frac{d}{dx}[f(g(x))] = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. By the Power Rule, noting that \(\frac{1}{\pi+1}\) is a constant:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = 7x^6 + \sqrt{7} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. By the Product Rule \((uv)' = u'v + uv'\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} \frac{dp}{d\theta} = 2\cos\theta - 2\theta\sin\theta \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. By the Quotient Rule \(\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)' = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} \frac{dr}{d\theta} = \frac{(-\sin\theta)(1-\cos\theta) - (1+\cos\theta)(\sin\theta)}{(1-\cos\theta)^2} \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \frac{dr}{d\theta} = \frac{-\sin\theta + \sin\theta\cos\theta - \sin\theta - \sin\theta\cos\theta}{(1-\cos\theta)^2} = \frac{-2\sin\theta}{(1-\cos\theta)^2} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    4. By the Chain Rule:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{du} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-u^2+\tan u}} \cdot (-2u + \sec^2 u) = \frac{\sec^2 u - 2u}{2\sqrt{1-u^2+\tan u}} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    5. By the Product Rule:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = (-1+6x)(2-9x) + (8-x+3x^2)(-9) \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = (-2 + 9x + 12x - 54x^2) + (-72 + 9x - 27x^2) = -81x^2 + 30x - 74 \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    6. By the Chain Rule (nested twice):
      \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = e^{\sin(x^2)} \cdot \cos(x^2) \cdot 2x = 2x\cos(x^2)e^{\sin(x^2)} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    7. By the Chain Rule:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{1-3x} \cdot (-3) = \frac{-3}{1-3x} = \frac{3}{3x-1} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Suppose that functions \(f\) and \(g\) and their derivatives with respect to \(x\) have the values shown in the table below:
    🔗
    \(x\) \(f(x)\) \(g(x)\) \(f'(x)\) \(g'(x)\)
    2 8 2 \(1/3\) \(-3\)
    3 3 \(-4\) \(2\pi\) 5
    Find the value of the following derivatives at the specified points:
    1. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[f(x) - \pi g(x)\right]\) at \(x = 3\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[\sqrt{2}f(x) \cdot g(x)\right]\) at \(x = 2\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right]\) at \(x = 3\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[(f \circ g)(x)\right]\) at \(x = 2\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    Convert the combinations into formula notations before substituting numbers. For part (d), write the Chain Rule expansion out explicitly: \(\frac{d}{dx}[f(g(x))] = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)\text{,}\) and find the inner value \(g(2)\) first.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. The derivative profile is \(f'(3) - \pi g'(3)\text{.}\) Substituting table values: \(2\pi - \pi(5) = -3\pi\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. By the Product Rule: \(\sqrt{2}\left(f'(2)g(2) + f(2)g'(2)\right)\text{.}\) Substituting values: \(\sqrt{2}\left((\frac{1}{3})(2) + (8)(-3)\right) = \sqrt{2}\left(\dfrac{2}{3} - 24\right) = -\dfrac{70\sqrt{2}}{3}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. By the Quotient Rule: \(\dfrac{f'(3)g(3) - f(3)g'(3)}{(g(3))^2}\text{.}\) Substituting values: \(\dfrac{(2\pi)(-4) - (3)(5)}{(-4)^2} = \dfrac{-8\pi - 15}{16}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. By the Chain Rule: \(f'(g(2)) \cdot g'(2)\text{.}\) Since \(g(2) = 2\text{,}\) this evaluates to \(f'(2) \cdot g'(2)\text{.}\) Substituting values: \(\left(\dfrac{1}{3}\right)(-3) = -1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    For \(y = \dfrac{1}{1+x}\text{,}\) find the first and second derivatives.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Rewrite the expression as \(y = (1+x)^{-1}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    First Derivative via Power/Chain Rules:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = -(1+x)^{-2} \cdot (1) = -\frac{1}{(1+x)^2} \end{equation*}
    Second Derivative:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{d}{dx}\left[-(1+x)^{-2}\right] = 2(1+x)^{-3} \cdot (1) = \frac{2}{(1+x)^3} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    Find the slope of the tangent line to the implicitly defined curve \(x^2y + \sin y = 2\pi\) at the point \(P(1, 2\pi)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Differentiate every term with respect to \(x\text{.}\) Treat \(y\) as an implicit function of \(x\text{,}\) which means appending a \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) factor whenever you differentiate a expression containing \(y\text{.}\) Don’t forget the Product Rule on \(x^2y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\text{,}\) using the Product Rule on the first term:
    \begin{equation*} \left(2xy + x^2\frac{dy}{dx}\right) + \cos y \frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \end{equation*}
    Isolate \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{dx}(x^2 + \cos y) = -2xy \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{-2xy}{x^2 + \cos y} \end{equation*}
    Evaluate at \(x = 1\) and \(y = 2\pi\text{,}\) noting that \(\cos(2\pi) = 1\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} m = \frac{-2(1)(2\pi)}{1^2 + \cos(2\pi)} = \frac{-4\pi}{1 + 1} = -2\pi \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    Use logarithmic differentiation to find the derivative of \(y\) with respect to \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} y = \dfrac{x\sqrt{x^2+1}}{3(x+1)^{2/3}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hint.
    Apply the natural log \(\ln\) to both sides first. Use the expansion properties: \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\) and \(\ln(\dfrac{a}{b}) = \ln a - \ln b\) to break down the right side into separate, simple logarithms before differentiating.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Take the natural logarithm of both sides and expand using log laws:
    \begin{equation*} \ln y = \ln(x) + \frac{1}{2}\ln(x^2+1) - \ln(3) - \frac{2}{3}\ln(x+1) \end{equation*}
    Differentiate implicitly with respect to \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{x} + \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2x}{x^2+1}\right) - 0 - \frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{1}{x+1}\right) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{x} + \frac{x}{x^2+1} - \frac{2}{3(x+1)} \end{equation*}
    Multiply by \(y\) to solve for \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{dx} = \left( \frac{x\sqrt{x^2+1}}{3(x+1)^{2/3}} \right) \left[ \frac{1}{x} + \frac{x}{x^2+1} - \frac{2}{3(x+1)} \right] \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6.

    Differentiate \(y = \tan^{-1}(x^2+3x)\) by modeling the expression geometrically as a right triangle using SOHCAHTOA.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Let \(y = \theta\text{.}\) The equation \(\theta = \tan^{-1}(x^2+3x)\) means that \(\tan(\theta) = \dfrac{x^2+3x}{1}\text{.}\) Use the TOA definition (\(\tan\theta = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Adjacent}}\)) to label a right triangle and determine its hypotenuse.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Method 1: To evaluate this using the geometric model, rewrite the inverse equation as a direct trigonometric relationship:
    \begin{equation*} \tan(y) = \frac{x^2+3x}{1} \end{equation*}
    According to SOHCAHTOA, \(\tan(y) = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Adjacent}}\text{.}\) We construct a right triangle with an angle \(y\text{,}\) setting \(\text{Opposite} = x^2+3x\) and \(\text{Adjacent} = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By the Pythagorean Theorem, the hypotenuse of this triangle is:
    \begin{equation*} \text{Hypotenuse} = \sqrt{(1)^2 + (x^2+3x)^2} = \sqrt{1 + (x^2+3x)^2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now, differentiate both sides of \(\tan(y) = x^2+3x\) implicitly with respect to \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \sec^2(y) \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = 2x + 3 \end{equation*}
    Isolate the derivative term:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2x+3}{\sec^2(y)} = (2x+3)\cos^2(y) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Using our geometric model triangle and the CAH rule (\(\cos(y) = \frac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}\)), read the value of \(\cos(y)\) directly from the diagram:
    \begin{equation*} \cos(y) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (x^2+3x)^2}} \end{equation*}
    Squaring both sides yields:
    \begin{equation*} \cos^2(y) = \frac{1}{1 + (x^2+3x)^2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Substitute this value back into the isolated derivative equation to reach the final answer:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = (2x+3) \cdot \left(\frac{1}{1 + (x^2+3x)^2}\right) = \frac{2x+3}{1 + x^4 + 6x^3 + 9x^2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Method 2: Using the rule \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\tan^{-1} u] = \dfrac{1}{1+u^2} \cdot u'\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{1}{1 + (x^2+3x)^2} \cdot (2x+3) = \dfrac{2x+3}{1 + x^4 + 6x^3 + 9x^2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    7.

    A police cruiser, approaching a right-angled intersection from the north, is chasing a speeding car that has turned the corner and is now moving straight east. When the cruiser is \(0.6\text{ mi}\) north of the intersection and the car is \(0.8\text{ mi}\) to the east, the police determine with radar that the distance between them and the car is increasing at \(20\text{ mph}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If the cruiser is moving at \(60\text{ mph}\) at the instant of measurement, what is the speed of the car?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Model this setup as a right triangle where \(x^2 + y^2 = z^2\text{.}\) Note carefully that because the police cruiser is moving closer to the intersection, its rate of change \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}\) must be entered as a negative value (\(-60\)).
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(y\) be the distance of the cruiser north of the intersection, \(x\) be the distance of the car east of the intersection, and \(z\) be the straight-line distance between them. By the Pythagorean Theorem:
    \begin{equation*} x^2 + y^2 = z^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    At the given instant, \(y = 0.6\) and \(x = 0.8\text{.}\) Thus:
    \begin{equation*} z = \sqrt{0.8^2 + 0.6^2} = \sqrt{0.64 + 0.36} = 1\text{ mi} \end{equation*}
    Because the cruiser is traveling south, its distance from the intersection is decreasing, so \(\dfrac{dy}{dt} = -60\text{ mph}\text{.}\) The radar shows \(\dfrac{dz}{dt} = 20\text{ mph}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Differentiating the primary equation implicitly with respect to time \(t\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 2x\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\frac{dy}{dt} = 2z\frac{dz}{dt} \implies x\frac{dx}{dt} + y\frac{dy}{dt} = z\frac{dz}{dt} \end{equation*}
    Substitute the known parameters to solve for the speed of the car, \(\dfrac{dx}{dt}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} (0.8)\frac{dx}{dt} + (0.6)(-60) = (1.0)(20) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 0.8\frac{dx}{dt} - 36 = 20 \implies 0.8\frac{dx}{dt} = 56 \implies \frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{56}{0.8} = 70\text{ mph} \end{equation*}
    The speeding car is traveling at \(70\text{ mph}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    8.

    Find the points on the graph of \(y = x^{3/2} - x^{1/2}\) at which the tangent line is parallel to the line \(y - x = 3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, identify the required target slope. Rewriting the line \(y - x = 3\) as \(y = x + 3\) shows its slope is \(m = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Next, compute the derivative of the target function to represent the general tangent slope:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3}{2}x^{1/2} - \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2} = \frac{3\sqrt{x}}{2} - \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} \end{equation*}
    Set this derivative equal to the target slope of \(1\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{3\sqrt{x}}{2} - \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} = 1 \end{equation*}
    Multiply the entire equation by the common denominator \(2\sqrt{x}\) (where \(x \neq 0\)):
    \begin{equation*} 3x - 1 = 2\sqrt{x} \implies 3x - 2\sqrt{x} - 1 = 0 \end{equation*}
    Let \(u = \sqrt{x}\text{.}\) This converts the relation into a manageable quadratic template:
    \begin{equation*} 3u^2 - 2u - 1 = 0 \implies (3u + 1)(u - 1) = 0 \end{equation*}
    This yields roots \(u = -\frac{1}{3}\) or \(u = 1\text{.}\) Because \(u = \sqrt{x} \ge 0\text{,}\) we discard the negative value. Thus, \(\sqrt{x} = 1 \implies x = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Substitute \(x = 1\) back into the original curve equation to find the corresponding \(y\)-coordinate:
    \begin{equation*} y = (1)^{3/2} - (1)^{1/2} = 1 - 1 = 0 \end{equation*}
    The only point on the graph with a tangent parallel to the given line is \((1, 0)\text{.}\)
