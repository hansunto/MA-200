    Section 3.6 L’Hopital’s Rule

    L’Hôpital’s Rule is a powerful calculus theorem used to evaluate limits that result in an indeterminate form like \(\dfrac{0}{0}\) or \(\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}\) upon direct substitution. Named after the French mathematician Guillaume de l’Hôpital, the rule allows you to bypass complex algebraic factoring or simplification by taking the derivatives of the numerator and denominator separately to find the limit.
    🔗
    The indeterminate forms: \(\dfrac{0}{0}\) and \(\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}\) and other indeterminate forms \(0^{0}\text{,}\) \(\infty^{0}\text{,}\) and \(1^{\infty}\text{.}\) We’d like to know the behavior of the limit:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 1}\dfrac{\ln x}{x-1} \end{equation*}
    This limit cannot be solved algebraically since the limit of the denominator is \(0\text{.}\) In fact, the numerator approaches \(\ln(1)=0\text{,}\) and \(\dfrac{0}{0}\) is not defined.
    🔗
    In general, if we have a limit of the form:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to a}\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)} \end{equation*}
    where \(f(x)\to 0\) and \(g(x)\to 0\) as \(x\to a\text{,}\) then this limit may or may not exist and is called an indeterminate form of type \(\dfrac{0}{0}\).
    🔗

    Example 275. Cancel Common Factors.

    Evaluate the following limit by canceling common factors:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 1}\frac{x^{2}-x}{x^{2}-1} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Direct substitution of \(x = 1\) results in the indeterminate form \(\frac{0}{0}\text{.}\) We can resolve this by factoring both the numerator and the denominator:
    \begin{align*} \lim_{x\to 1}\frac{x^{2}-x}{x^{2}-1} \amp = \lim_{x\to 1}\frac{x(x-1)}{(x-1)(x+1)}\\ \amp = \lim_{x\to 1}\frac{x}{x+1} \end{align*}
    Now, evaluating the limit by direct substitution yields:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{1+1} = \frac{1}{2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 276. Use a Geometric Argument.

    Evaluate the following limit using a geometric argument on the unit circle:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0}\dfrac{\sin x}{x}=1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Setting up the inequality based on these areas gives:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{1}{2}\sin x \lt \frac{1}{2}x \lt \frac{1}{2}\tan x \end{equation*}
    Multiplying all parts by \(2\) simplifies the inequality to:
    \begin{equation*} \sin x \lt x \lt \tan x=\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since \(0 \lt x \lt \dfrac{\pi}{2}\text{,}\) \(\sin x \gt 0\text{.}\) We can divide the entire inequality chain by \(\sin x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 1 \lt \dfrac{x}{\sin x} \lt \frac{1}{\cos x} \end{equation*}
    Taking the reciprocal of each term reverses the inequalities:
    \begin{equation*} 1 \gt \dfrac{\sin x}{x} \gt \cos x \end{equation*}
    which can be rewritten in standard order as:
    \begin{equation*} \cos x \lt \dfrac{\sin x}{x} \lt 1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now, applying the Squeeze Theorem as \(x \to 0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to 0} \cos x = 1 \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{x \to 0} 1 = 1 \end{equation*}
    Since both the lower and upper bounds approach \(1\text{,}\) the middle limit is forced to match:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    If we have a limit of the form
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)} \end{equation*}
    where both \(f(x)\) and \(g(x) \to \pm \infty\text{,}\) then the limit may or may not exist and is called an indeterminate form of type \(\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}\).
    🔗

    Example 278. Finding Limits at Infinity.

    Evaluate the following limit by dividing the numerator and denominator by the highest power of \(x\) that occurs in the denominator:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{x^{2}-1}{2x^{2}+1} \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    The highest power of \(x\) in the denominator is \(x^2\text{.}\) We divide every term in both the numerator and denominator by \(x^2\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{x^{2}-1}{2x^{2}+1} \amp= \lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\dfrac{x^2}{x^2}-\dfrac{1}{x^2}}{\dfrac{2x^2}{x^2}+\dfrac{1}{x^2}}\\ \amp= \lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{1-\dfrac{1}{x^2}}{2+\dfrac{1}{x^2}} \end{align*}
    As \(x \to \infty\text{,}\) the term \(\dfrac{1}{x^2} \to 0\text{.}\) Applying the limit laws gives:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp= \dfrac{1-0}{2+0}\\ \amp= \dfrac{1}{2} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 279. L’Hospital’s Rule.

    Suppose \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable and \(g'(x) \neq 0\) on an open interval \(I\) that contains \(a\) (except possibly at \(a\)). Suppose that
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=0 \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{x\to a}g(x)=0 \end{equation*}
    or that
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=\pm \infty \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{x\to a}g(x)=\pm \infty\text{.} \end{equation*}
    (In other words, we have an indeterminate form of type \(0/0\) or \(\infty/\infty\)). Then
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to a}\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\lim_{x\to a}\dfrac{f'(x)}{g'(x)} \end{equation*}
    if the limit on the right side exists (or is \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\)).
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    We will establish the proof for the special case where \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = 0\) and \(\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = 0\text{,}\) using a right-hand limit \(x \to a^+\text{.}\) We can extend or define \(f(a) = 0\) and \(g(a) = 0\text{,}\) making both functions continuous at \(x = a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let \(x\) be a point in \(I\) such that \(x > a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Because \(f\) and \(g\) are continuous on the closed interval \([a, x]\) and differentiable on the open interval \((a, x)\text{,}\) we can apply Mean Value Theorem (the next section). This guarantees that there exists a number \(c\) strictly between \(a\) and \(x\) (\(a < c < x\)) such that:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)} = \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{g(x) - g(a)} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since we defined \(f(a) = 0\) and \(g(a) = 0\text{,}\) this identity simplifies to:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)} = \frac{f(x) - 0}{g(x) - 0} = \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now, we take the limit as \(x \to a^+\text{.}\) As \(x\) approaches \(a\) from the right, the value of \(c\) (which is squeezed between \(a\) and \(x\)) is forced to approach \(a\) as well (\(c \to a^+\)). Therefore:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to a^+} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to a^+} \frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)} = \lim_{c \to a^+} \frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A symmetrical argument applies to the left-hand limit as \(x \to a^-\text{.}\) Combining these results completes the proof for two-sided limits. \(\qquad \blacksquare\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 280.

    The limit notation \(x \to a\) can be replaced by \(x \to a^{+}\text{,}\) \(x \to a^{-}\text{,}\) \(x \to \infty\text{,}\) or \(x \to -\infty\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    For the special case where \(f(a)=g(a)=0\text{,}\) if \(f'\) and \(g'\) are continuous and \(g'(a) \neq 0\text{,}\) then it is easy to see why L’Hospital’s Rule is true.
    🔗

    Example 281. Evaluating Indeterminate Forms Using L’Hospital’s Rule.

    Evaluate each of the following limits:
    🔗

    (a)

    \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1}\dfrac{\ln x}{x-1}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This limit has the indeterminate form of type \(0/0\text{.}\) Applying L’Hospital’s Rule:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 1}\frac{\ln x}{x-1} = \lim_{x\to 1}\frac{1/x}{1} = \frac{1}{1} = 1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to \infty}\dfrac{e^{x}}{x^{2}}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This limit has the indeterminate form of type \(\infty/\infty\text{.}\) Applying L’Hospital’s Rule twice:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to \infty}\frac{e^{x}}{x^{2}} = \lim_{x\to \infty}\frac{e^{x}}{2x} = \lim_{x\to \infty}\frac{e^{x}}{2} = \infty \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to \pi^-}\dfrac{\sin x}{1-\cos x}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Direct substitution yields \(\dfrac{\sin(\pi)}{1-\cos(\pi)} = \dfrac{0}{1 - (-1)} = \dfrac{0}{2} = 0\text{.}\) This is not an indeterminate form, so L’Hospital’s Rule is not needed.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+}x\ln x\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This limit has the indeterminate form of type \(0 \cdot (-\infty)\text{.}\) Rewrite it as a quotient:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0^+}x\ln x=\lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{\ln x}{1/x} \end{equation*}
    Now it is of type \(-\infty/\infty\text{.}\) Applying L’Hospital’s Rule:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{1/x}{-1/x^2} = \lim_{x\to 0^+}(-x) = 0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to (\pi/2)^-}(\sec x-\tan x)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This limit has the indeterminate form of type \(\infty - \infty\text{.}\) Rewrite using a common denominator:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to (\pi/2)^-}\left(\frac{1}{\cos x} - \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\right) = \lim_{x\to (\pi/2)^-}\frac{1-\sin x}{\cos x} \end{equation*}
    Now it is of type \(0/0\text{.}\) Applying L’Hospital’s Rule:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to (\pi/2)^-}\frac{-\cos x}{-\sin x} = \frac{0}{1} = 0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+}x^{x}\) using the identity \([f(x)]^{g(x)}=e^{g(x)\ln f(x)}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This limit has the indeterminate form of type \(0^0\text{.}\) We can rewrite the function using the natural exponential and logarithmic identity \(x^x = e^{\ln(x^x)} = e^{x \ln x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Because the exponential function is continuous, we can move the limit inside the exponent:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0^+} x^x = \lim_{x\to 0^+} e^{x \ln x} = e^{\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+} x \ln x} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Next, we evaluate the limit in the exponent. This forms an indeterminate product of type \(0 \cdot (-\infty)\text{.}\) We transform it into a fraction of type \(-\infty/\infty\) to apply L’Hospital’s Rule:
    \begin{align*} \lim_{x\to 0^+} x \ln x \amp= \lim_{x\to 0^+} \frac{\ln x}{1/x}\\ \amp= \lim_{x\to 0^+} \frac{\frac{d}{dx}[\ln x]}{\frac{d}{dx}[1/x]}\\ \amp= \lim_{x\to 0^+} \frac{1/x}{-1/x^2}\\ \amp= \lim_{x\to 0^+} (-x)\\ \amp= 0 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Finally, substitute this value back into the original exponent expression:
    \begin{equation*} e^{\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+} x \ln x} = e^0 = 1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (g)

    \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+}(1+\sin 2x)^{\cot x}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This limit has the indeterminate form of type \(1^\infty\text{.}\) We rewrite the expression as a power of \(e\) using the property \(y = e^{\ln y}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} (1+\sin 2x)^{\cot x} = e^{\ln\left[(1+\sin 2x)^{\cot x}\right]} = e^{\cot x \ln(1+\sin 2x)} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Moving the limit into the exponent gives:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0^+} (1+\sin 2x)^{\cot x} = e^{\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+} \cot x \ln(1+\sin 2x)} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We now evaluate the limit of the exponent. Since \(\cot x = \dfrac{1}{\tan x}\text{,}\) we rewrite the product as a quotient to form an indeterminate type of \(0/0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0^+} \cot x \ln(1+\sin 2x) = \lim_{x\to 0^+} \frac{\ln(1+\sin 2x)}{\tan x} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Applying L’Hospital’s Rule by differentiating the numerator and the denominator yields:
    \begin{align*} \lim_{x\to 0^+} \frac{\ln(1+\sin 2x)}{\tan x} \amp= \lim_{x\to 0^+} \frac{\frac{2\cos 2x}{1+\sin 2x}}{\sec^2 x}\\ \amp= \frac{\frac{2\cos(0)}{1+\sin(0)}}{\sec^2(0)}\\ \amp= \frac{\frac{2(1)}{1+0}}{1}\\ \amp= 2 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Substituting the evaluated limit back into the exponent gives our final result:
    \begin{equation*} e^{\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+} \cot x \ln(1+\sin 2x)} = e^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 282. Additional Indeterminate Forms Practice.

    Before applying L’Hospital’s Rule, you must first verify that the limit produces an indeterminate form of type \(0/0\) or \(\infty/\infty\) via direct substitution. For other indeterminate types—such as products (\(0 \cdot \infty\)), differences (\(\infty - \infty\)), or powers (\(1^\infty, 0^0, \infty^0\))—you must first use algebraic strategies, common denominators, or logarithmic identities to rewrite the expression as a quotient before differentiating.
    🔗
    Evaluate each of the following limits using L’Hospital’s Rule where appropriate:
    🔗

    (a)

    \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\dfrac{e^{x}+e^{-x}-2}{1-\cos 2x}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Direct substitution yields the indeterminate form \(0/0\text{.}\) Applying L’Hospital’s Rule gives:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0}\dfrac{e^x - e^{-x}}{2\sin 2x} \end{equation*}
    This still evaluates to \(0/0\text{.}\) Applying L’Hospital’s Rule a second time yields:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0}\dfrac{e^x + e^{-x}}{4\cos 2x} = \dfrac{1+1}{4(1)} = \dfrac{2}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to (\pi/2)^-}\dfrac{4\tan x}{1+\sec x}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This limit yields the indeterminate form \(\infty/\infty\text{.}\) Applying L’Hospital’s Rule:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to (\pi/2)^-}\dfrac{4\sec^2 x}{\sec x \tan x} = \lim_{x\to (\pi/2)^-}\dfrac{4\sec x}{\tan x} \end{equation*}
    Rewriting the trigonometric functions in terms of sine and cosine:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to (\pi/2)^-} \dfrac{4}{\cos x} \cdot \dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \lim_{x\to (\pi/2)^-} \dfrac{4}{\sin x} = \dfrac{4}{1} = 4 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\dfrac{e^{x}+e^{-x}}{x^{2}}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Direct substitution yields \(\dfrac{1+1}{0} = \dfrac{2}{0}\text{.}\) Since this is not an indeterminate form, L’Hospital’s Rule does not apply. Because the numerator is positive and the denominator approaches zero through positive values:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0}\dfrac{e^{x}+e^{-x}}{x^{2}} = \infty \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+}\dfrac{e^{-1/x}}{x}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This limit yields the indeterminate form \(0/0\text{.}\) To avoid a worsening algebraic loop, we rewrite it as an \(\infty/\infty\) form by shifting the exponential term to the denominator:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0^+}\dfrac{1/x}{e^{1/x}} \end{equation*}
    Applying L’Hospital’s Rule:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0^+}\dfrac{-1/x^2}{-1/x^2 \cdot e^{1/x}} = \lim_{x\to 0^+}\dfrac{1}{e^{1/x}} = 0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+}x^{2}\ln x\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This limit has the indeterminate product form \(0 \cdot (-\infty)\text{.}\) Converting it to a quotient:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0^+}\dfrac{\ln x}{1/x^2} \end{equation*}
    Applying L’Hospital’s Rule:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0^+}\dfrac{1/x}{-2/x^3} = \lim_{x\to 0^+}\left(-\dfrac{x^2}{2}\right) = 0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to (\pi/2)^-}(2x-\pi)\sec x\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This limit has the indeterminate product form \(0 \cdot \infty\text{.}\) Rewriting using cosines:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to (\pi/2)^-}\dfrac{2x-\pi}{\cos x} \end{equation*}
    Applying L’Hospital’s Rule to this \(0/0\) form:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to (\pi/2)^-}\dfrac{2}{-\sin x} = \dfrac{2}{-1} = -2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (g)

    \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+}(1+3x)^{1/(2x)}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This limit has the indeterminate form \(1^\infty\text{.}\) Rewriting as an exponential base \(e\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0^+} e^{\dfrac{1}{2x}\ln(1+3x)} = e^{\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+} \dfrac{\ln(1+3x)}{2x}} \end{equation*}
    Applying L’Hospital’s Rule to the exponent fraction:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0^+}\dfrac{\dfrac{3}{1+3x}}{2} = \dfrac{3}{2} \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the complete evaluation yields \(e^{3/2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (h)

    \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+}\left(\dfrac{1}{e^{x}-1}-\dfrac{1}{x}\right)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This limit presents an indeterminate difference of type \(\infty - \infty\text{.}\) Combining fractions under a single denominator:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0^+}\dfrac{x - (e^x - 1)}{x(e^x - 1)} = \lim_{x\to 0^+}\dfrac{x - e^x + 1}{xe^x - x} \end{equation*}
    This forms a \(0/0\) expression. Applying L’Hospital’s Rule:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0^+}\dfrac{1 - e^x}{xe^x + e^x - 1} \end{equation*}
    Evaluating again yields \(0/0\text{.}\) Applying L’Hospital’s Rule a second time:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0^+}\dfrac{-e^x}{xe^x + e^x + e^x} = \dfrac{-1}{0 + 1 + 1} = -\dfrac{1}{2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
