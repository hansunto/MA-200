    Section 3.3 Concavity and Curve Sketching

    This section explores how the rate of change of the first derivative determines the bending behavior, or concavity, of a function’s graph.
    🔗

    Definition 248. Concavity.

    Suppose that \(f\) is a function whose derivative \(f'\) exists at every point in an open interval \(I\text{.}\) Then:
    1. \(f\) is concave up on the interval \(I\text{,}\) if \(f'\) is increasing over \(I\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(f\) is concave down on the interval \(I\text{,}\) if \(f'\) is decreasing over \(I\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 249. The Concavity Test.

    Let \(f\) be a twice-differentiable function on an interval \(I\text{.}\)
    1. If \(f''(x) \gt 0\) for all \(x\) in \(I\text{,}\) then the graph of \(f\) is concave up on \(I\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. If \(f''(x) \lt 0\) for all \(x\) in \(I\text{,}\) then the graph of \(f\) is concave down on \(I\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 250.

    If \(f(x)=x^{3}+x^{2}-5x-5\text{,}\) determine intervals on which the graph of \(f\) is concave up or down.
    🔗
    Solution.
    To apply the Concavity Test, we find the first and second derivatives of \(f(x)\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} f'(x) = 3x^2 + 2x - 5 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} f''(x) = 6x + 2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We find where the second derivative is zero to identify potential inflection points:
    \begin{equation*} 6x + 2 = 0 \implies x = -\frac{1}{3} \end{equation*}
    This point divides the domain into two open intervals. We construct a sign chart to analyze \(f''(x)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    Interval \(\left(-\infty, -\dfrac{1}{3}\right)\) \(\left(-\dfrac{1}{3}, \infty\right)\)
    Sign of \(f''(x)\) \(\lt 0\) \(\gt 0\)
    Concavity of \(f\) Concave Down \(\cap\) Concave Up \(\cup\)
    Therefore, the graph of \(f\) is concave down on the interval \(\left(-\infty, -\dfrac{1}{3}\right)\) and concave up on the interval \(\left(-\dfrac{1}{3}, \infty\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 252. Point of Inflection.

    A point of inflection is a point across which the direction of concavity changes. A point \((c,f(c))\) on the graph of \(f\) is a point of inflection if:
    1. \(f\) is continuous at \(c\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. There is an open interval \((a,b)\) containing \(c\) such that the graph is concave up on \((a,c)\) and concave down on \((c,b)\text{,}\) or vice versa.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 253. The Second Derivative Test.

    Suppose that \(f\) is a function for which \(f'(x)\) exists for every \(x\) in an open interval \((a,b)\) contained in its domain, and that there is a critical point \(c\) in \((a,b)\) for which \(f'(c)=0\text{.}\) Then:
    1. \(f(c)\) is a local minimum if \(f''(c) \gt 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(f(c)\) is a local maximum if \(f''(c) \lt 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    The test is inconclusive if \(f''(c)=0\) or if \(f''(c)\) does not exist. In such cases, the First Derivative Test must be used instead.
    🔗
    🔗

    Finding Points of Inflection.

    If a function \(f\) has a point of inflection, it occurs at a point \(x_{0}\) in its domain where either:
    \begin{equation*} f''(x_{0})=0 \quad \text{or} \quad f''(x_{0}) \text{ does not exist.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 254.

    If \(f(x)=12+2x^{2}-x^{4}\text{,}\) use the second derivative test to find the local extrema of \(f\text{.}\) Determine the concavity and find the points of inflection.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We begin by computing the first and second derivatives of the function:
    \begin{equation*} f'(x) = 4x - 4x^3 = 4x(1 - x^2) = 4x(1-x)(1+x) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} f''(x) = 4 - 12x^2 = 4(1 - 3x^2) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Part 1: Local Extrema (Second Derivative Test)
    🔗
    Setting \(f'(x) = 0\) gives our critical numbers: \(x = 0\text{,}\) \(x = 1\text{,}\) and \(x = -1\text{.}\) We evaluate the sign of \(f''(x)\) at each critical number:
    • At \(x = 0\text{:}\) \(f''(0) = 4(1 - 0) = 4 \gt 0\text{.}\) By the Second Derivative Test, \(f\) has a local minimum at \(x = 0\text{.}\) The local minimum value is \(f(0) = 12\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • At \(x = 1\text{:}\) \(f''(1) = 4 - 12(1)^2 = -8 \lt 0\text{.}\) By the Second Derivative Test, \(f\) has a local maximum at \(x = 1\text{.}\) The local maximum value is \(f(1) = 12 + 2(1)^2 - (1)^4 = 13\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • At \(x = -1\text{:}\) \(f''(-1) = 4 - 12(-1)^2 = -8 \lt 0\text{.}\) By the Second Derivative Test, \(f\) has a local maximum at \(x = -1\text{.}\) The local maximum value is \(f(-1) = 12 + 2(-1)^2 - (-1)^4 = 13\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Part 2: Concavity and Points of Inflection
    🔗
    To find potential inflection points, we set \(f''(x) = 0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 4 - 12x^2 = 0 \implies x^2 = \frac{1}{3} \implies x = \pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \end{equation*}
    These values divide the real line into three open intervals. We construct a sign chart to evaluate the concavity of \(f\text{:}\)
    🔗
    Interval \(\left(-\infty, -\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\) \(\left(-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\) \(\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, \infty\right)\)
    Sign of \(f''(x)\) \(\lt 0\) \(\gt 0\) \(\lt 0\)
    Concavity of \(f\) Concave Down \(\cap\) Concave Up \(\cup\) Concave Down \(\cap\)
    The graph of \(f\) is concave down on \(\left(-\infty, -\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\) and \(\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, \infty\right)\text{,}\) and concave up on \(\left(-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Since the concavity changes directions at both \(x = -\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\) and \(x = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\text{,}\) both locations are inflection points. We evaluate their corresponding \(y\)-values:
    \begin{equation*} f\left(\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right) = 12 + 2\left(\frac{1}{3}\right) - \left(\frac{1}{9}\right) = 12 + \frac{6}{9} - \frac{1}{9} = 12 + \frac{5}{9} = \frac{113}{9} \end{equation*}
    Thus, the points of inflection are \(\left(-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, \dfrac{113}{9}\right)\) and \(\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, \dfrac{113}{9}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
