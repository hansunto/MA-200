    Section 4.5 Area Between Curves

    Theorem 357. Area Between Bounded Graphs.

    If \(f\) and \(g\) are continuous and \(f(x) \geq g(x)\) for every \(x\) in \([a,b]\text{,}\) then the area \(A\) of the region bounded by the graphs of \(f\) and \(g\text{,}\) and the vertical lines \(x=a\) and \(x=b\) is:
    \begin{equation*} A = \int^b_a [f(x) - g(x)] \, dx \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Guidelines for Finding the Area of a Region.

    1. Sketch the region, labeling the upper boundary \(y=f(x)\) and the lower boundary \(y=g(x)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the smallest value \(x=a\) and the largest value \(x=b\) for points \((x,y)\) in the region.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Express the area as \(\int_{a}^{b} [f(x) - g(x)] \, dx\) and evaluate the definite integral.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 358. Area Bounded by Exponential and Linear Functions.

    Find the area of the region bounded above by \(y=e^x\text{,}\) bounded below by \(y=x\text{,}\) and bounded on the sides by \(x=0\) and \(x=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    On the interval \(\text{,}\) the exponential function \(y = e^x\) is always greater than or equal to the linear function \(y = x\text{.}\) Setting up the integral using the upper function minus the lower function yields:
    \begin{align*} A \amp = \int^{1}_{0} (e^x - x) \, dx\\ \amp = \left[ e^x - \frac{1}{2}x^2 \right]^{1}_{0}\\ \amp = \left( e^1 - \frac{1}{2}(1)^2 \right) - \left( e^0 - \frac{1}{2}(0)^2 \right)\\ \amp = \left( e - \frac{1}{2} \right) - (1 - 0) = e - \frac{3}{2} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 360. Area Enclosed by Two Parabolas.

    Find the area of the region enclosed by the parabolas \(y=x^2\) and \(y=2x-x^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, we find the limits of integration by locating the intersection points of the two curves. We set the quadratic equations equal to each other:
    \begin{align*} x^2 \amp = 2x - x^2\\ 2x^2 - 2x \amp = 0 \implies 2x(x - 1) = 0 \end{align*}
    This gives intersection boundaries at \(x = 0\) and \(x = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    On the interval \(\text{,}\) \(2x - x^2 \geq x^2\text{.}\) Therefore, \(f(x) = 2x - x^2\) is the upper boundary and \(g(x) = x^2\) is the lower boundary. We evaluate the definite integral:
    \begin{align*} A \amp = \int^{1}_{0} \left[ (2x - x^2) - x^2 \right] \, dx\\ \amp = \int^{1}_{0} (2x - 2x^2) \, dx\\ \amp = \left[ x^2 - \frac{2}{3}x^3 \right]^{1}_{0} = 1 - \frac{2}{3} = \frac{1}{3} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 362. Area Between a Parabola and a Square Root Function.

    Find the area of the region bounded by the graphs of the equations \(y=x^{2}\) and \(y=\sqrt{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, we find the limits of integration by finding the points of intersection for the two curves. We set the equations equal to each other:
    \begin{align*} x^2 \amp = \sqrt{x}\\ x^4 \amp = x \quad \text{(squaring both sides)}\\ x^4 - x \amp = 0\\ x(x^3 - 1) \amp = 0 \end{align*}
    This gives real intersection boundaries at \(x = 0\) and \(x = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    On the interval \([0,1]\text{,}\) evaluating a test point like \(x = 0.25\) shows that \(\sqrt{0.25} = 0.5 \gt (0.25)^2 = 0.0625\text{.}\) Therefore, \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\) is the upper boundary and \(g(x) = x^2\) is the lower boundary (see Figure 363).
    🔗
    We set up and evaluate the definite integral using the power rule:
    \begin{align*} A \amp = \int^{1}_{0} \left( \sqrt{x} - x^2 \right) \, dx\\ \amp = \int^{1}_{0} \left( x^{1/2} - x^2 \right) \, dx\\ \amp = \left[ \frac{2}{3}x^{3/2} - \frac{x^3}{3} \right]^{1}_{0}\\ \amp = \left( \frac{2}{3}(1)^{3/2} - \frac{(1)^3}{3} \right) - (0 - 0)\\ \amp = \frac{2}{3} - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{3} \end{align*}
    🔗
    The exact area of the bounded region is \(\dfrac{1}{3}\) square units.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 364. Area Bounded by Three Intersecting Curves.

    Find the area of the region bounded by the graphs of the equations \(y-x=6\text{,}\) \(y-x^{3}=0\text{,}\) and \(2y+x=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, we rewrite each equation explicitly in terms of \(y\text{:}\)
    • Line 1 (\(L_1\)): \(y = x + 6\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Cubic Curve (\(C\)): \(y = x^3\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Line 2 (\(L_2\)): \(y = -\frac{1}{2}x\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Next, we find the three intersection points to define our boundaries:
    1. Intersection of \(L_1\) and \(L_2\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} x + 6 = -\frac{1}{2}x \implies \frac{3}{2}x = -6 \implies x = -4 \end{equation*}
      This gives the vertex point \((-4, 2)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Intersection of \(C\) and \(L_2\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} x^3 = -\frac{1}{2}x \implies x\left(x^2 + \frac{1}{2}\right) = 0 \implies x = 0 \end{equation*}
      This gives the vertex point \((0, 0)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Intersection of \(L_1\) and \(C\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} x^3 = x + 6 \implies x^3 - x - 6 = 0 \implies (x-2)(x^2+2x+3) = 0 \implies x = 2 \end{equation*}
      This gives the vertex point \((2, 8)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    As seen in Figure 365, the upper boundary of the region is always the line \(y = x + 6\text{.}\) However, the lower boundary changes at \(x = 0\text{.}\) Therefore, the area must be split into two separate definite integrals:
    \begin{equation*} A = \int_{-4}^{0} \left[ (x+6) - \left(-\frac{1}{2}x\right) \right] \, dx + \int_{0}^{2} \left[ (x+6) - x^3 \right] \, dx \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We evaluate the first area component (\(A_1\)) from \(x = -4\) to \(x = 0\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} A_1 \amp = \int_{-4}^{0} \left( \frac{3}{2}x + 6 \right) \, dx = \left[ \frac{3}{4}x^2 + 6x \right]_{-4}^{0}\\ \amp = (0) - \left( \frac{3}{4}(-4)^2 + 6(-4) \right)\\ \amp = -\left( \frac{3}{4}(16) - 24 \right) = -(12 - 24) = 12 \end{align*}
    🔗
    We evaluate the second area component (\(A_2\)) from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 2\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} A_2 \amp = \int_{0}^{2} \left( x + 6 - x^3 \right) \, dx = \left[ \frac{1}{2}x^2 + 6x - \frac{x^4}{4} \right]_{0}^{2}\\ \amp = \left( \frac{1}{2}(2)^2 + 6(2) - \frac{2^4}{4} \right) - (0)\\ \amp = (2 + 12 - 4) = 10 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Summing the two subregions yields the total area of the bounded space:
    \begin{equation*} A = A_1 + A_2 = 12 + 10 = 22 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore, the total area of the bounded region is exactly \(22\) square units.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 366. Area Bounded by Sine and Cosine Functions.

    Find the area of the region bounded by the curves \(y=\sin x\text{,}\) \(y=\cos x\text{,}\) \(x=0\text{,}\) and \(x=\pi/2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    To find the total area of the bounded region, we first find where the two curves intersect on the interval \([0, \pi/2]\text{.}\) We set the equations equal to each other:
    \begin{equation*} \sin x = \cos x \implies \tan x = 1 \implies x = \frac{\pi}{4} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This intersection splits our interval into two symmetric subregions where the boundaries swap positions:
    • On \([0, \pi/4]\text{,}\) \(\cos x \geq \sin x\) (Cosine is the upper boundary).
      🔗
      🔗
    • On \([\pi/4, \pi/2]\text{,}\) \(\sin x \geq \cos x\) (Sine is the upper boundary).
      🔗
      🔗
    We express the total area \(A\) as the sum of two separate definite integrals:
    \begin{equation*} A = \int_{0}^{\pi/4} (\cos x - \sin x) \, dx + \int_{\pi/4}^{\pi/2} (\sin x - \cos x) \, dx \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We evaluate the first definite integral component:
    \begin{align*} \int_{0}^{\pi/4} (\cos x - \sin x) \, dx \amp = \left[ \sin x + \cos x \right]_{0}^{\pi/4}\\ \amp = \left( \sin\frac{\pi}{4} + \cos\frac{\pi}{4} \right) - (\sin 0 + \cos 0)\\ \amp = \left( \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \right) - (0 + 1) = \sqrt{2} - 1 \end{align*}
    🔗
    We evaluate the second definite integral component:
    \begin{align*} \int_{\pi/4}^{\pi/2} (\sin x - \cos x) \, dx \amp = \left[ -\cos x - \sin x \right]_{\pi/4}^{\pi/2}\\ \amp = \left( -\cos\frac{\pi}{2} - \sin\frac{\pi}{2} \right) - \left( -\cos\frac{\pi}{4} - \sin\frac{\pi}{4} \right)\\ \amp = (0 - 1) - \left( -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} - \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \right) = -1 + \sqrt{2} = \sqrt{2} - 1 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Summing the two subregions yields the final area value:
    \begin{equation*} A = (\sqrt{2} - 1) + (\sqrt{2} - 1) = 2\sqrt{2} - 2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore, the exact area of the bounded region is \(2\sqrt{2} - 2\) square units.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 368. Area Enclosed by a Parabola and a Line.

    Find the area of the region between \(y=x^{2}\) and \(y=2x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First, we find the limits of integration by finding the points of intersection for the two curves. We set the quadratic and linear expressions equal to each other:
    \begin{align*} x^2 \amp = 2x\\ x^2 - 2x \amp = 0\\ x(x - 2) \amp = 0 \end{align*}
    This gives intersection boundaries at \(x = 0\) and \(x = 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    On the interval \([0, 2]\text{,}\) testing a point like \(x = 1\) shows that \(2(1) \gt (1)^2\text{.}\) Therefore, the line \(f(x) = 2x\) is the upper boundary and the parabola \(g(x) = x^2\) is the lower boundary.
    🔗
    We set up and evaluate the definite integral using the standard power rule:
    \begin{align*} A \amp = \int_{0}^{2} (2x - x^2) \, dx\\ \amp = \left[ x^2 - \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_{0}^{2}\\ \amp = \left( (2)^2 - \frac{(2)^3}{3} \right) - (0 - 0)\\ \amp = \left( 4 - \frac{8}{3} \right)\\ \amp = \frac{12}{3} - \frac{8}{3} = \frac{4}{3} \end{align*}
    🔗
    The exact area of the enclosed region is \(\frac{4}{3}\) square units.
