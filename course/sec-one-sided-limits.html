    Section One-sided Limits

    Insight 23.

    The phrase “from either side” used in the preceding definition is very important. The notation we use \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x)\) to indicate the limit from the right and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x)\) to indicate the limit from the left. If both limits exist and are the same, then we say the limit exists.
    🔗
    🔗
    Let \(f(x) = x - 5\text{.}\) First let’s look at the meaning of “the limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches 5 from the right.” This is written as:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to 5^+} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 5^+} (x - 5) \end{equation*}
    In \(x \to 5^+\text{,}\) the \(+\) means we are approaching 5 from the positive side. This side is to the right of 5 on a number line. Now as \(x\) approaches 5 from the right, note that \(x - 5\) is always positive. The limit from the right is of course zero, but the fact that \(x - 5\) is always positive will be used later on.
    🔗
    Now, “the limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches 5 from the left” is the same notion. It is written as:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to 5^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 5^-} (x - 5) \end{equation*}
    The \(-\) sign means we are approaching 5 from the negative side. This side is to the left of 5 on a number line. Note that as \(x\) approaches 5 from the left, \(x - 5\) is always negative. The limit from the left is of course zero, but we will make use of the fact that \(x - 5\) is always negative later on.
    🔗

    Theorem 24. Two-sided Limit.

    A function \(f\) has the limit \(L\) as \(x\) approaches \(a\) if the limit from the left exists and the limit from the right exists and both limits are \(L\text{.}\) That is:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L \iff \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 25.

    Show that \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{x}\) does not exist.
    🔗
    Solution.
    To show that the two-sided limit does not exist, we evaluate the one-sided limits:
    • As \(x\) approaches \(0\) from the right (\(x \to 0^+\)), the values of \(x\) are positive and decreasing, so \(\frac{1}{x}\) increases without bound:
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{1}{x} = \infty \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • As \(x\) approaches \(0\) from the left (\(x \to 0^-\)), the values of \(x\) are negative and increasing toward \(0\text{,}\) so \(\frac{1}{x}\) decreases without bound:
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to 0^-} \frac{1}{x} = -\infty \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Since the one-sided limits are not equal (and do not approach a finite real number), the two-sided limit \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{x}\) does not exist.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 26.

    Consider the function \(H\) defined as follows:
    \begin{equation*} H(x)= \begin{cases} 2x+2 \amp \text{for } x \lt 1 \\ 2x-2 \amp \text{for } x \ge 1 \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    Graph the function and find each of the following limits, if they exist:
    1. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\lim_{x \rightarrow 1} H(x)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\lim_{x \rightarrow -3} H(x)\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Check one-sided limits at \(x=1\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to 1^-} H(x) = 2(1)+2 = 4 \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to 1^+} H(x) = 2(1)-2 = 0 \end{equation*}
      Since \(4 \neq 0\text{,}\) the limit does not exist (DNE).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. At \(x = -3\text{,}\) we only use the first piece (\(x \lt 1\)):
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to -3} (2x+2) = 2(-3)+2 = -4 \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 28.

    Sketch the graph of an example of a function \(f\) that satisfies all the given conditions.
    🔗
    1. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x)=2\text{,}\) \(\quad \displaystyle\lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x)=-2\text{,}\) \(\quad f(1)=2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0^-} f(x)=1\text{,}\) \(\quad \displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0^+} f(x)=-1\text{,}\) \(\quad \displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2^-} f(x)=0\text{,}\) \(\quad \displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x)=1\text{,}\) \(\quad f(2)=1\text{,}\) and \(f(0)\) is undefined.
      🔗
      🔗
    Solution.
    Possible solutions for the graphs:
    🔗
    1. Conditions: \(\lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x)=2\text{,}\) \(\lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x)=-2\text{,}\) and \(f(1)=2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Conditions: \(\lim_{x \to 0^-} f(x)=1\text{,}\) \(\lim_{x \to 0^+} f(x)=-1\text{,}\) \(\lim_{x \to 2^-} f(x)=0\text{,}\) \(\lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x)=1\text{,}\) \(f(2)=1\text{,}\) and \(f(0)\) is undefined.
      🔗

    Checkpoint 31.

    If \(f(x)=\displaystyle \frac{|x|}{x}\text{,}\) find:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to 0^-} f(x), \quad \lim_{x \to 0^+} f(x), \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{x \to 0} f(x) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    We rewrite the function by using the definition of absolute value:
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{x}{x} = 1 \amp \text{if } x \gt 0 \\ \frac{-x}{x} = -1 \amp \text{if } x \lt 0 \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    Now we evaluate the limits:
    • From the left: \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 0^-} (-1) = -1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • From the right: \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0^+} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 0^+} (1) = 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Two-sided limit: Since \(-1 \neq 1\text{,}\) the limit \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} f(x)\) does not exist (DNE).
      🔗
      🔗

    Special Trigonometric Limit.

    \begin{equation*} \lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin \theta}{\theta} = 1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 32.

    Show that \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0} \frac{\sin 2x}{5x}=\frac{2}{5}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We use the special limit \(\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin \theta}{\theta} = 1\text{.}\) Multiply the numerator and denominator to match the argument \(2x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0} \frac{\sin 2x}{5x} = \frac{1}{5} \lim_{x\to 0} \frac{\sin 2x}{x} \cdot \frac{2}{2} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \frac{2}{5} \lim_{x\to 0} \frac{\sin 2x}{2x} = \frac{2}{5}(1) = \frac{2}{5} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 33.

    Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to \infty} x\sin\frac{1}{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(\theta = 1/x\text{.}\) As \(x \to \infty\text{,}\) \(\theta \to 0^+\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to \infty} x \sin(1/x) = \lim_{\theta \to 0^+} \frac{1}{\theta} \sin \theta = \lim_{\theta \to 0^+} \frac{\sin \theta}{\theta} = 1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 34.

    Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0} \frac{\cos x-1}{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Direct substitution results in the indeterminate form \(0/0\text{.}\) To evaluate this, we multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate \(\cos x + 1\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0} \frac{\cos x - 1}{x} \cdot \frac{\cos x + 1}{\cos x + 1} = \lim_{x\to 0} \frac{\cos^2 x - 1}{x(\cos x + 1)} \end{equation*}
    Using the Pythagorean identity \(\cos^2 x - 1 = -\sin^2 x\text{,}\) we rewrite the limit:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0} \frac{-\sin^2 x}{x(\cos x + 1)} = \lim_{x\to 0} \left( \frac{\sin x}{x} \cdot \frac{-\sin x}{\cos x + 1} \right) \end{equation*}
    Applying the special limit \(\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} (1) \cdot \left( \frac{-\sin(0)}{\cos(0) + 1} \right) = 1 \cdot \frac{0}{1+1} = 0 \end{equation*}
    Thus, \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0} \frac{\cos x-1}{x} = 0\text{.}\)
