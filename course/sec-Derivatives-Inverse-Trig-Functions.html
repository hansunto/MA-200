    Section Derivatives of Inverse Trigonometric Functions

    In this section, we develop the differentiation formulas for the inverse trigonometric functions. By applying implicit differentiation to our geometric and algebraic definitions of these functions, we can find their rates of change explicitly in terms of the independent variable \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 186. Inverse Trigonometric Functions.

    1. \(y=\sin^{-1}x=\arcsin x\) is the number in \([-\pi/2,\pi/2]\) for which \(\sin y=x\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(y=\cos^{-1}x=\arccos x\) is the number in \([0,\pi]\) for which \(\cos y=x\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(y=\tan^{-1}x=\arctan x\) is the number in \((-\pi/2,\pi/2)\) for which \(\tan y=x\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 187.

    Find the exact angle in radians for each of the following expressions:
    🔗

    (a)

    \(\sin^{-1}(1/\sqrt{2})\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(y = \sin^{-1}(1/\sqrt{2})\text{.}\) By definition, this means:
    \begin{equation*} \sin y = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \end{equation*}
    where \(y\) must lie in the restricted principal interval \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\text{.}\) Looking at the standard unit circle in the first quadrant, the unique angle matching this condition is:
    \begin{equation*} y = \frac{\pi}{4} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    \(\cos^{-1}(-1/2)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(y = \cos^{-1}(-1/2)\text{.}\) This is equivalent to solving:
    \begin{equation*} \cos y = -\frac{1}{2} \end{equation*}
    where \(y\) must lie in the restricted principal interval \([0, \pi]\text{.}\) Since the cosine value is negative, the angle must terminate in the second quadrant. The reference angle whose cosine equals \(1/2\) is \(\pi/3\text{.}\) Subtracting this reference angle from \(\pi\) yields:
    \begin{equation*} y = \pi - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{2\pi}{3} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    \(\tan^{-1}(-1/\sqrt{3})\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(y = \tan^{-1}(-1/\sqrt{3})\text{.}\) This implies:
    \begin{equation*} \tan y = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3} \end{equation*}
    where \(y\) must lie in the open principal interval \((-\pi/2, \pi/2)\text{.}\) Since the tangent value is negative, the angle must terminate in the fourth quadrant. The reference angle whose tangent is \(1/\sqrt{3}\) is \(\pi/6\text{.}\) Expressing this within the required fourth-quadrant range yields a negative rotation:
    \begin{equation*} y = -\frac{\pi}{6} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 188.

    Find each of the following limits:
    🔗

    (a)

    \(\displaystyle \lim_{x\to 1^-} \sin^{-1}x\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The function \(f(x) = \sin^{-1}x\) is continuous on its closed domain \([-1, 1]\text{.}\) Therefore, we evaluate the left-hand limit approaching \(1\) by direct substitution:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 1^-} \sin^{-1}x = \sin^{-1}(1) \end{equation*}
    Since \(\sin(\pi/2) = 1\) and \(\pi/2\) falls within the principal range interval \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\text{,}\) we get:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 1^-} \sin^{-1}x = \frac{\pi}{2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    \(\displaystyle \lim_{x\to \infty} \tan^{-1}x\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    As \(x\) grows infinitely large without bound (\(x \to \infty\)), we are looking for the angle \(y\) in the principal open interval \((-\pi/2, \pi/2)\) whose tangent value approaches infinity. Geometrically, this corresponds to the location of the right-hand horizontal asymptote on the graph of the arctangent function:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to \infty} \tan^{-1}x = \frac{\pi}{2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    \(\displaystyle \lim_{x\to \infty} \sec^{-1}x\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    By definition, the inverse secant function can be rewritten using the reciprocal identity:
    \begin{equation*} \sec^{-1}x = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \end{equation*}
    Taking the limit as \(x \to \infty\) means the inner component \(\frac{1}{x} \to 0^+\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to \infty} \sec^{-1}x = \lim_{x\to \infty} \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) = \cos^{-1}(0) \end{equation*}
    Since \(\cos(\pi/2) = 0\) and \(\pi/2\) falls within the principal range interval \([0, \pi]\text{,}\) we conclude:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to \infty} \sec^{-1}x = \frac{\pi}{2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Derivatives of Inverse Trigonometric Functions.

    Let \(u\) be a differentiable function of \(x\text{.}\) The derivatives of the primary inverse trigonometric functions are defined as follows:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}(\arcsin u) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^2}} \frac{du}{dx}, \quad |u| \lt 1 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}(\arccos u) = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^2}} \frac{du}{dx}, \quad |u| \lt 1 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}(\arctan u) = \frac{1}{1+u^2} \frac{du}{dx} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Derivation of the Derivative of \(\arcsin x\)

    To find the derivative of \(y = \arcsin x\text{,}\) we begin with its defining algebraic constraint:
    \begin{equation*} \sin y = x \quad \text{for } -\frac{\pi}{2} \le y \le \frac{\pi}{2} \end{equation*}
    Differentiating both sides implicitly with respect to \(x\) yields:
    \begin{equation*} \cos y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = 1 \end{equation*}
    Isolating the derivative component gives us:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{\cos y} \end{equation*}
    Because \(y\) is restricted to the interval \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\text{,}\) the cosine value remains non-negative (\(\cos y \ge 0\)). We apply the fundamental Pythagorean trigonometric identity \(\sin^2 y + \cos^2 y = 1\) to isolate \(\cos y\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \cos y = \sqrt{1 - \sin^2 y} \end{equation*}
    Substituting \(\sin y = x\) directly back into the square root expression completes our calculation:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}(\arcsin x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 189.

    Differentiate each of the following expressions:
    🔗

    (a)

    \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}(\sin^{-1}x^2)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Method 1: Formula Method (Chain Rule) We apply the inverse sine derivative rule with the chain rule, setting the inner function \(u = x^2\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}(\sin^{-1}u) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^2}} \cdot \frac{du}{dx} \end{equation*}
    Substituting \(u = x^2\) and its inner derivative \(\frac{du}{dx} = 2x\) yields:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}(\sin^{-1}x^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-(x^2)^2}} \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(x^2) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^4}} \cdot 2x = \frac{2x}{\sqrt{1-x^4}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Method 2: Geometric SOHCAHTOA and Implicit Differentiation Let \(y = \sin^{-1}x^2\text{,}\) which means \(\sin y = \frac{x^2}{1}\text{.}\) Using SOHCAHTOA, \(\sin y = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}\text{.}\) We sketch a reference right triangle with an angle \(y\text{,}\) opposite side \(x^2\text{,}\) and hypotenuse \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By the Pythagorean theorem, the adjacent side is \(\sqrt{1^2 - (x^2)^2} = \sqrt{1-x^4}\text{.}\) From the triangle, we find \(\cos y = \frac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Hypotenuse}} = \frac{\sqrt{1-x^4}}{1}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now differentiate \(\sin y = x^2\) implicitly with respect to \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \cos y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = 2x \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2x}{\cos y} \end{equation*}
    Substitute \(\cos y = \sqrt{1-x^4}\) into the expression:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2x}{\sqrt{1-x^4}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}(\tan^{-1}5x^3)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Method 1: Formula Method (Chain Rule) We apply the inverse tangent derivative rule combined with the chain rule, setting the inner function \(u = 5x^3\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}(\tan^{-1}u) = \frac{1}{1+u^2} \cdot \frac{du}{dx} \end{equation*}
    Substituting \(u = 5x^3\) and computing its inner derivative \(\frac{du}{dx} = 15x^2\) gives:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}(\tan^{-1}5x^3) = \frac{1}{1+(5x^3)^2} \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(5x^3) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} = \frac{1}{1+25x^6} \cdot 15x^2 = \frac{15x^2}{1+25x^6} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Method 2: Geometric SOHCAHTOA and Implicit Differentiation Let \(y = \tan^{-1}5x^3\text{,}\) meaning \(\tan y = \frac{5x^3}{1} = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Adjacent}}\text{.}\) We sketch a reference right triangle with angle \(y\text{,}\) opposite side \(5x^3\text{,}\) and adjacent side \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The hypotenuse is \(\sqrt{1^2 + (5x^3)^2} = \sqrt{1+25x^6}\text{.}\) From the triangle, we find \(\sec y = \frac{\text{Hypotenuse}}{\text{Adjacent}} = \sqrt{1+25x^6}\text{,}\) which means \(\sec^2 y = 1+25x^6\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now differentiate \(\tan y = 5x^3\) implicitly with respect to \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \sec^2 y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = 15x^2 \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{15x^2}{\sec^2 y} \end{equation*}
    Substitute \(\sec^2 y = 1+25x^6\) into the expression:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{15x^2}{1+25x^6} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}(\sec^{-1}(x^2+3))\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Method 1: Formula Method (Chain Rule) We apply the inverse secant derivative rule with the chain rule, setting the inner function \(u = x^2+3\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}(\sec^{-1}u) = \frac{1}{|u|\sqrt{u^2-1}} \cdot \frac{du}{dx} \end{equation*}
    Substituting \(u = x^2+3\) and its inner derivative \(\frac{du}{dx} = 2x\) gives:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}(\sec^{-1}(x^2+3)) = \frac{1}{|x^2+3|\sqrt{(x^2+3)^2-1}} \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(x^2+3) \end{equation*}
    Since \(x^2+3 \gt 0\) for all real numbers, we can safely remove the absolute value bars:
    \begin{equation*} = \frac{2x}{(x^2+3)\sqrt{(x^4+6x^2+9)-1}} = \frac{2x}{(x^2+3)\sqrt{x^4+6x^2+8}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Method 2: Geometric SOHCAHTOA and Implicit Differentiation Let \(y = \sec^{-1}(x^2+3)\text{,}\) meaning \(\sec y = \frac{x^2+3}{1} = \frac{\text{Hypotenuse}}{\text{Adjacent}}\text{.}\) We sketch a reference right triangle with angle \(y\text{,}\) hypotenuse \(x^2+3\text{,}\) and adjacent side \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The opposite side length matches \(\sqrt{(x^2+3)^2 - 1^2} = \sqrt{x^4+6x^2+8}\text{.}\) From the triangle, we find \(\tan y = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Adjacent}} = \sqrt{x^4+6x^2+8}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now differentiate \(\sec y = x^2+3\) implicitly with respect to \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \sec y \tan y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = 2x \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2x}{\sec y \tan y} \end{equation*}
    Substitute \(\sec y = x^2+3\) and \(\tan y = \sqrt{x^4+6x^2+8}\) back into the expression:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2x}{(x^2+3)\sqrt{x^4+6x^2+8}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises Exercises on Inverse Trigonometric Derivatives (Geometric Method)

    Differentiate each of the following functions with respect to its independent variable using right triangle geometry, SOHCAHTOA relationships, and implicit differentiation.
    🔗

    1.

    \(y=\cos^{-1}(1/x)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Rewrite the function as \(\cos y = \frac{1}{x}\text{.}\) Using SOHCAHTOA, \(\cos y = \frac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}\text{.}\) We envision a right triangle with an angle \(y\text{,}\) adjacent side \(1\text{,}\) and hypotenuse \(x\text{.}\) By the Pythagorean theorem, the opposite side is \(\sqrt{x^2 - 1^2} = \sqrt{x^2 - 1}\text{.}\) From this geometry, we find \(\sin y = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Hypotenuse}} = \frac{\sqrt{x^2 - 1}}{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now, differentiate \(\cos y = x^{-1}\) implicitly with respect to \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} -\sin y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = -x^{-2} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{x^2 \sin y} \end{equation*}
    Substitute \(\sin y = \frac{\sqrt{x^2 - 1}}{x}\) back into the equation:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{x^2 \left(\frac{\sqrt{x^2 - 1}}{x}\right)} = \frac{1}{x\sqrt{x^2 - 1}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    \(y=\sin^{-1}(\sqrt{2}t)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Rewrite the function as \(\sin y = \frac{\sqrt{2}t}{1}\text{.}\) Using SOHCAHTOA, \(\sin y = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}\text{.}\) We envision a right triangle with an angle \(y\text{,}\) opposite side \(\sqrt{2}t\text{,}\) and hypotenuse \(1\text{.}\) By the Pythagorean theorem, the adjacent side is \(\sqrt{1^2 - (\sqrt{2}t)^2} = \sqrt{1 - 2t^2}\text{.}\) From this geometry, we find \(\cos y = \frac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Hypotenuse}} = \frac{\sqrt{1 - 2t^2}}{1} = \sqrt{1 - 2t^2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now, differentiate \(\sin y = \sqrt{2}t\) implicitly with respect to \(t\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \cos y \cdot \frac{dy}{dt} = \sqrt{2} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{\cos y} \end{equation*}
    Substitute \(\cos y = \sqrt{1 - 2t^2}\) back into the equation:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{1 - 2t^2}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    \(y=\csc^{-1}(x/2)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Rewrite the function as \(\csc y = \frac{x}{2}\text{.}\) Using SOHCAHTOA, \(\csc y = \frac{\text{Hypotenuse}}{\text{Opposite}}\text{.}\) We envision a right triangle with an angle \(y\text{,}\) hypotenuse \(x\text{,}\) and opposite side \(2\text{.}\) By the Pythagorean theorem, the adjacent side is \(\sqrt{x^2 - 2^2} = \sqrt{x^2 - 4}\text{.}\) From this geometry, we find \(\cot y = \frac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Opposite}} = \frac{\sqrt{x^2 - 4}}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now, differentiate \(\csc y = \frac{x}{2}\) implicitly with respect to \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} -\csc y \cot y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{2} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{1}{2 \csc y \cot y} \end{equation*}
    Substitute \(\csc y = \frac{x}{2}\) and \(\cot y = \frac{\sqrt{x^2 - 4}}{2}\) back into the equation:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{1}{2 \left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \left(\frac{\sqrt{x^2 - 4}}{2}\right)} = -\frac{2}{x\sqrt{x^2 - 4}} \end{equation*}
    Note: For negative values of \(x\text{,}\) absolute value bars \(|x|\) are required to ensure a proper positive range for the derivative of a cosecant root, yielding \(-\frac{2}{|x|\sqrt{x^2 - 4}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    \(y=\tan^{-1}(x/2)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Rewrite the function as \(\tan y = \frac{x}{2}\text{.}\) Using SOHCAHTOA, \(\tan y = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Adjacent}}\text{.}\) We envision a right triangle with an angle \(y\text{,}\) opposite side \(x\text{,}\) and adjacent side \(2\text{.}\) By the Pythagorean theorem, the hypotenuse is \(\sqrt{2^2 + x^2} = \sqrt{4 + x^2}\text{.}\) From this geometry, we find \(\sec y = \frac{\text{Hypotenuse}}{\text{Adjacent}} = \frac{\sqrt{4 + x^2}}{2}\text{,}\) which means \(\sec^2 y = \frac{4 + x^2}{4}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now, differentiate \(\tan y = \frac{x}{2}\) implicitly with respect to \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \sec^2 y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{2} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{2 \sec^2 y} \end{equation*}
    Substitute \(\sec^2 y = \frac{4 + x^2}{4}\) back into the equation:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{2 \left(\frac{4 + x^2}{4}\right)} = \frac{1}{\frac{4 + x^2}{2}} = \frac{2}{4 + x^2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    \(y=\ln(x^2+4)-x\tan^{-1}(x/2)\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We differentiate each part separately, applying the product rule to the second term:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}\left[\ln(x^2+4)\right] - \frac{d}{dx}\left[x\tan^{-1}(x/2)\right] \end{equation*}
    The derivative of the first term via the natural log chain rule is \(\frac{2x}{x^2+4}\text{.}\) For the second term, we use the product rule: \((1)\cdot\tan^{-1}(x/2) + x\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\left[\tan^{-1}(x/2)\right]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To geometric-style differentiate the inner item \(\theta = \tan^{-1}(x/2)\text{,}\) rewrite it as \(\tan \theta = \frac{x}{2}\) where \(\text{Opposite}=x\) and \(\text{Adjacent}=2\text{.}\) The hypotenuse is \(\sqrt{4+x^2}\text{,}\) meaning \(\sec \theta = \frac{\sqrt{4+x^2}}{2}\) and \(\sec^2 \theta = \frac{4+x^2}{4}\text{.}\) Implicitly differentiating yields:
    \begin{equation*} \sec^2 \theta \cdot \frac{d\theta}{dx} = \frac{1}{2} \implies \frac{d\theta}{dx} = \frac{1}{2\left(\frac{4+x^2}{4}\right)} = \frac{2}{4+x^2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Combine both main parts back together and subtract:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2x}{x^2+4} - \left(\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) + \frac{2x}{4+x^2}\right) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2x}{x^2+4} - \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) - \frac{2x}{x^2+4} = -\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6.

    \(y=\cos^{-1}(e^{-t})\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Rewrite the function as \(\cos y = \frac{e^{-t}}{1}\text{.}\) Using SOHCAHTOA, \(\cos y = \frac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}\text{.}\) We envision a right triangle with an angle \(y\text{,}\) adjacent side \(e^{-t}\text{,}\) and hypotenuse \(1\text{.}\) By the Pythagorean theorem, the opposite side length matches \(\sqrt{1^2 - (e^{-t})^2} = \sqrt{1 - e^{-2t}}\text{.}\) From this geometry, we find \(\sin y = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Hypotenuse}} = \frac{\sqrt{1 - e^{-2t}}}{1} = \sqrt{1 - e^{-2t}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now, differentiate \(\cos y = e^{-t}\) implicitly with respect to \(t\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} -\sin y \cdot \frac{dy}{dt} = -e^{-t} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{e^{-t}}{\sin y} \end{equation*}
    Substitute \(\sin y = \sqrt{1 - e^{-2t}}\) back into the equation:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{e^{-t}}{\sqrt{1 - e^{-2t}}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
